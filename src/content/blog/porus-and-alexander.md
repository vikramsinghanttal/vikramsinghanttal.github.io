Grow up in Punjab or Haryana and you inherit the story before you can evaluate it. A great foreign conqueror, undefeated across half the known world, comes down through the passes, and on the bank of one of our rivers he meets a local king who will not kneel. They fight. The foreigner wins, barely, and then, moved by the beaten king's bearing, he gives him back his kingdom and more. When asked how he wishes to be treated, the captured king says: as a king treats a king. It is a beautiful story. It is told at prize distributions and in schoolbooks and in films. And the honest thing I can tell you, having gone back to the sources as an adult, is that we do not know if the best part of it is true.

I want to take the story apart carefully, not to spoil it, but because I think the way we know it is as interesting as what happened, and a good deal more useful.

## The one thing we can be fairly sure of

In the summer of 326 BCE, Alexander of Macedon reached the Hydaspes, the river we now call the Jhelum, in the Punjab. On the far bank waited a king the Greeks called Poros, ruler of the land between the Jhelum and the Chenab. A battle was fought. Alexander won it. Afterward he did not depose Poros but confirmed him as a ruler under Macedonian overlordship, and then marched on east until his own army stopped him at another river and made him turn around. Within three years Alexander was dead in Babylon and never saw Macedon again.

That skeleton is about as solid as ancient history gets. Almost everything hung on the skeleton, though, comes to us through a specific and lopsided set of witnesses, and once you see the lopsidedness you cannot unsee it.

## Whose pen, whose war

Here is the fact that reorganized the whole episode for me. There is no contemporary Indian account of Alexander. None. The most world-shaking military tour in the ancient imagination, the campaign that Europe has retold for two thousand years, left essentially no ripple in the Indian written record of its own time. Our knowledge of the Battle of the Hydaspes is Greek and Roman, top to bottom.

And even the Greek and Roman sources we have are late. The eyewitnesses, men like Ptolemy who became a king in Egypt, and Aristobulus who served on the campaign, wrote accounts that are now lost. What survives is the second layer, historians who read those lost books and rewrote them centuries afterward. Arrian, our most sober guide, wrote his Anabasis roughly four hundred years after the battle. Plutarch, Quintus Curtius Rufus, Diodorus, and Justin range from careful to frankly novelistic, and they contradict one another on numbers, on tactics, on who did what. When you read that Porus had two hundred elephants, or that the river was crossed at a wooded bend eighteen miles upstream under cover of a thunderstorm, you are reading a Roman-era reconstruction of a lost memoir of a battle in Punjab. That is a long chain, and every link is a Greek hand.

I do not say this to throw the sources away. Arrian in particular is a serious historian, and the broad outline he gives is coherent and probably close to right. I say it because the identity of the narrator shapes the tale in ways we should keep in view. The Greeks needed Porus to be magnificent. A conqueror is only as impressive as the enemies he overcomes, and by the time Alexander reached India his men were exhausted and his legend needed feeding. A noble, gigantic Indian king who fought brilliantly and lost with dignity is exactly the opponent the Alexander story requires. That does not make Porus fictional. It means the portrait was painted by people with a stake in how it looked.

## The battle itself, as the Greeks describe it

Take the sources on their own terms and the battle is genuinely gripping, and, I think, genuinely instructive.

The Jhelum in the monsoon is not a river you simply ford. It runs high, brown, and fast, and Porus had arranged his forces along the eastern bank precisely so that any crossing would be contested at the waterline, where an army is most helpless. Alexander, according to Arrian, solved this the way he solved most things, by refusing the obvious. He made a show of camping and waiting, lulling Porus with feints, and then on a wild, rainy night he took a picked force far upstream to a wooded bend and crossed there, out of sight, while the storm covered the noise.

What followed on the eastern plain is one of the more interesting battles of the ancient world because of the elephants. Porus fronted his line with war elephants, and the Macedonian cavalry, which was the sharp edge of every Alexandrian victory, could not easily be thrown against them, because horses will not charge elephants. The elephants did real damage, breaking the phalanx in places, and the Greek accounts do not hide how hard the fighting was. Alexander's answer was to have his infantry harass the elephants with javelins and axes, wounding and maddening them until they turned and trampled their own side, which is the recurring tragedy of the war elephant: a weapon that becomes a danger to whoever deployed it the moment it panics. The chariots that Porus also fielded bogged down in the monsoon mud, useless. In the end the discipline and mobility of Alexander's force ground down a larger, more static army, and Porus, wounded and still fighting from his elephant when most of his men were gone, was among the last to yield.

Somewhere around this campaign Alexander's famous horse Bucephalus died, and he founded a city named for the animal. Even the conqueror's grief here is a river-country detail.

## The line we cannot verify

Now the part everyone remembers. Porus, wounded and captured, is brought before Alexander, who asks how he wishes to be treated. Porus answers: as a king. Or in the fuller version, as one king should treat another. Alexander, delighted, restores his kingdom and enlarges it, and the two become allies.

I would love this to be exactly true. It may be close to true. Arrian and Plutarch both carry a version of it, so it is not some medieval embroidery; it goes back into the tradition reasonably far. But I have to be straight about what it is. It is precisely the sort of noble-captive exchange that ancient biographers reached for again and again, a set piece designed to show the greatness of the victor through the greatness of the vanquished. The neat symmetry, the perfect line, the instant reconciliation: these are the fingerprints of a story being shaped for effect, not a court transcript. What we can say with confidence is the outcome, that Alexander kept Porus in power rather than replacing him, which was in any case sound policy for a conqueror who needed a loyal local ruler to hold a distant frontier. Whether the marvelous sentence was ever actually spoken, I do not know, and neither does anyone else, and I think we should keep the sentence while admitting the not-knowing. A legend can be worth preserving and still be a legend.

## The river where the conqueror stopped

The part of the story we tell least, and should tell most, is what happened next, because it complicates the whole triumphant frame.

Having won at the Jhelum, Alexander pushed east, and reached the Hyphasis, the river we call the Beas. There his army stopped. Not defeated. It simply refused to go on. They had been marching and fighting for the better part of a decade, they were far from home in a land whose scale kept expanding in front of them, and they had heard what waited beyond: the great kingdoms of the Ganga plain, the Nanda empire with its own vast army and its own thousands of elephants. The soldiers had had enough. In the accounts, an officer named Coenus speaks for them, respectfully and immovably, and Alexander, who had never been told no by his own men, retreats to his tent and sulks, and then gives way. He turned around at the Beas. He never crossed it.

Sit with that. The undefeated conqueror was not stopped by an enemy at all. He was stopped by his own soldiers' unwillingness and by the sheer, deterrent size of what lay ahead. The return journey down the Indus and through the Gedrosian desert was brutal, killing more of his men than any battle had, and he was dead in Babylon not long after. From the Indian side, the whole tremendous invasion touched a corner of the northwest and receded like a flood, leaving a few Greek garrisons that were soon absorbed, and within a generation Chandragupta Maurya built an empire across exactly the lands Alexander's men had been afraid to enter.

So who won? Alexander won the battle at the river. But he did not take India, he did not hold what he crossed, he lost the argument with his own army, and he died young and far away. Porus lost the battle and kept his kingdom, his throne, and, thanks entirely to his enemies' historians, an immortal reputation for dignity. The record and the memory point in different directions, and the gap between them is the whole lesson.

## Who gets to author history

I keep coming back to that gap, because I think it is the most important thing this old story has to teach, and it has almost nothing to do with elephants.

We did not write this down. The people who lived on these rivers, whose king it was, whose land it was, left no account of the most famous thing that ever happened on their doorstep, and so we see it forever through the eyes of the people who came to take it. Every heroic adjective attached to Porus was chosen by a Greek. Every number, every quotation, every noble gesture reaches us pre-interpreted by the other side. It is a permanent reminder that history is not what happened. History is what somebody bothered to record and someone else bothered to keep, and the two can be very far from the same thing.

I do not think the lesson is cynicism, that it is all propaganda and nothing can be known. Arrian was trying to get it right, and much of what he gives us is probably reliable. The lesson is humility about sources, and something more personal underneath it. The Indian silence was not innocence. It was, at least in part, a civilization that valued the recitation of dharma and lineage and myth over the dry keeping of dated political events, and that choice had a price paid in the currency of memory: we forfeited the right to tell our own version of our own defeat.

There is dignity in the Porus of the story, real dignity, the refusal to grovel before overwhelming force. I would not want to lose it. But the deeper dignity, the harder one, is the discipline of writing down what happened to you while it is happening, plainly and honestly, so that when the conquerors ride away, the account of the day is not left entirely in their saddlebags. Porus, if he lived as the Greeks say he lived, understood how a king should carry himself in defeat. What we did not understand, for far too long, is that carrying yourself well is not enough. You also have to be the one holding the pen.
